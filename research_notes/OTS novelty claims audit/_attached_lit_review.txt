Literature Review — Confidence-Adaptive Multi-Channel EW
Oct 21, 2026 · @Ehsan
II. Related Work
The system proposed in this paper sits at the confluence of four research programs that have, until now, matured largely in isolation: cognitive electronic warfare (EW) and learning-based jamming of frequency-hopping (FH) links; prediction and cryptanalytic recovery of pseudo-random hopping sequences; distribution-free uncertainty quantification via conformal methods; and game-theoretic, worst-case evaluation of adversarial radio-frequency (RF) policies. The contribution of this work is not a new algorithm within any one of these programs but the coupling of a calibrated hop predictor to a budgeted, threshold-aware multi-tone power allocation under an explicit, adversarially valid risk guarantee. Reviewing each program in turn clarifies both the mature foundations we build upon and the specific seam that no prior effort has closed. The two lead subsections (II-A, II-B) survey the electronic-attack and sequence-prediction literatures most directly adjacent to our problem; the two supporting subsections (II-C, II-D) survey the statistical and evaluation machinery that makes the flagship guarantee possible. Each subsection closes with an explicit statement of the gap it leaves open.
A. Cognitive Electronic Warfare and Reinforcement-Learning Jamming of Frequency-Hopping Links
The modern treatment of electronic attack as a closed-loop, sense–learn–adapt problem descends from the cognitive-radio and cognitive-radar programs, in which a node observes the spectrum, infers the state of an adversary whose model is unknown, and adapts its own waveform or channel in response [1]. In the anti-jamming direction this lineage is now mature. Han, Xiao, and Poor [2] introduced a deep Q-network (DQN) formulation of two-dimensional anti-jamming in which a secondary user jointly exploits spread spectrum and mobility to evade a smart jammer without knowing its model, and Liu et al. [3] showed that a raw spectrum-waterfall image could drive a deep anti-jamming Q-network directly. A substantial body of work has since refined the communicator side: Qi et al. [4] learn wideband hopping strategies by DQN, Li et al. [5] introduce a dual-action network over a joint transmit-frequency/power action space and verify it in field trials, and Zhang and Wu [6] trade anti-jamming performance against synchronization overhead through block-shifted hopping patterns. Recent fast-frequency-hopping work by Cheng et al. [7] adds an available-frequency-set abstraction and a multi-action deep recurrent Q-network to respect the per-hop latency budget. A 2024 survey by Jia et al. [8] consolidates this game-theory-and-reinforcement-learning view of anti-jamming defense.
The game-theoretic treatment of the jammer–transmitter interaction is older and denser. Hanawal, Abdel-Rahman, and Krunz [9] model joint FH and rate adaptation against a power-constrained reactive-sweep jammer as a constrained zero-sum Markov game and derive a deterministic threshold defense. Jia et al. [10] cast discrete-power anti-jamming as a Stackelberg game and prove asymptotic convergence of a hierarchical power-control algorithm under imperfect information, while Gao et al. [11] give necessary and sufficient Nash-equilibrium conditions for a bimatrix FH game. Li et al. [12] extend this to a Bayesian Stackelberg game that captures the jammer&apos;s observation errors and bounded rationality. A recurring theme across these works is that the formal guarantee stops at equilibrium existence, deterministic optimality within the assumed model, or asymptotic convergence — not at distribution-free risk control or worst-case exploitability of a deployed policy.
On the jammer side, which is where our system operates, the literature is markedly thinner and almost entirely recent. The closest prior art is the work of Rao et al., who treat jamming resource allocation against FHSS as a high-dimensional combinatorial problem: one variant allocates partial-band noise-jamming center frequency and bandwidth across jamming nodes with an asynchronous advantage actor-critic (A3C) and an LSTM feature extractor [13], and another uses trust-region and meta-trust-region policy optimization to generalize across changing hop-set distributions [14]. Zhang and Wu [15] push further toward multi-tone operation with a one-leader multi-follower Stackelberg formulation of cooperative jamming, coordinating frequency and power across several jammers, and explicitly argue that single-jammer, single-domain attacks are losing effectiveness against learning-based FH defenses. Pourranjbar et al. [16], [17] study reactive single-tone and multitone jammers from the defender&apos;s perspective, proposing reinforcement-learning deception schemes and deriving a statistical lower bound on received power. Classical jamming-type trade-offs — barrage versus spot, follower versus open-loop — recur throughout [9], [18], with the follower jammer&apos;s sensing advantage and the hop-rate/switching-overhead tension repeatedly noted.
Gap. Across this literature three things are consistently absent. First, the dominant action space is a single choice — a channel, a hop pattern, or a scalar power level — rather than a continuous power budget simultaneously apportioned across many energized tones; the handful of multi-channel jamming works [13], [14], [15] allocate bandwidth or coordinate nodes but do not solve a threshold-aware knapsack over channels. Second, no prior jamming system couples a calibrated, uncertainty-quantified hop prediction to the allocation decision: predictors, where present, emit point actions or unquantified value estimates, not risk-controlled predictive distributions. Third, the guarantees reported are model-based equilibrium or convergence statements, never distribution-free bounds on realized jamming effect that hold for arbitrary, adversarially adaptive hop sequences. The present work targets precisely this seam.
B. Frequency-Hopping Prediction and Sequence Cryptanalysis
Predicting the next channel of an unknown hopper rests on two distinct traditions: exact algebraic recovery of the generating mechanism, and statistical or learned forecasting when no exact structure is available. The algebraic tradition is classical and sharp. The Berlekamp–Massey algorithm recovers the shortest linear-feedback shift register (LFSR) consistent with a sequence from roughly twice its linear complexity in clean, consecutive outputs [19], and this remains the benchmark for breaking an unobfuscated maximal-length hopper; Strickland et al. [20] exploit exactly this to reverse-engineer LFSR-based hopping jammers from a handful of samples. When a nonlinear Boolean function filters the register, correlation attacks apply: Siegenthaler&apos;s original attack and the fast correlation attacks of Meier and Staffelbach [21] recover the register state by exploiting a statistical bias, and a long line of algebraic and fast-correlation refinements [22], [23] has followed. For non-shift-register generators, linear congruential generators are predictable from short outputs via lattice reduction, and weakly keyed generators fall to bounded seed/key-space search; the common thread is that each attack degrades sharply with missed detections and nonlinear obfuscation, and that a properly keyed cryptographic generator defeats all of them, leaving the predictor no better than uniform. Recent work continues to sharpen these tools for the non-cooperative setting — Kim et al. [24], for instance, improve spreading-code estimation by exploiting the LFSR linear relation through Berlekamp–Massey.
When exact recovery is impossible, the field turns to statistical and machine-learning prediction. Early approaches model the hop stream with Markov or n-gram transition statistics and hidden Markov models (HMMs). Neural sequence models now dominate: Li et al. [25], [26] apply long short-term memory (LSTM) networks to reconnaissance and prediction of non-cooperative FH frequencies; Zhu et al. [27] combine a residual CNN with a gated recurrent unit and transfer learning to recover hopping sequences robustly across hop-sets; Deng et al. [28] cast FH prediction as a state-space-model problem solved by a Bayesian-optimized temporal convolutional network; and Deng et al. [29] address nonlinear (chaotic) hop prediction with a homotopy-HMM when prior information is scarce. The broader shift from recurrent and convolutional backbones toward structured state-space models — S4 [30] and the selective-state-space Mamba and Mamba-2 architectures [31], [32] — is beginning to reach RF sequence modeling for its linear-time inference on long, noisy histories, which is the backbone our encoder adopts.
A parallel thread addresses blind characterization: estimating hop timing, rate, dwell, and the frequency set, and sorting interleaved emitters, directly from intercepted signals. Time-frequency, spatial, and sparse methods estimate multi-emitter FH parameters [33], [34], including under partial-band observation [35] and with missing observations via atomic-norm methods [36], and learning-based extractors now perform fully-blind multi-source sorting [37]. These establish that hop observations in a realistic non-cooperative setting are gap-ridden and noisy — the regime any downstream predictor must tolerate.
Gap. Published FH predictors share three limiting assumptions. They are fit per sequence or per emitter — trained or tuned on the target hopper&apos;s own data — rather than pretrained to generalize zero-shot across an adversary library. They assume clean, consecutive outputs, so that missed detections and false hops degrade them sharply, whereas realistic passive interception is gap-ridden [33]–[37]. And, decisively for our purpose, they emit a point prediction or hard classification rather than a calibrated predictive distribution with quantified, sequence-valid uncertainty that a risk-controlled allocator can consume. No prior FH predictor is simultaneously zero-shot, gap-robust, and calibrated.
C. Distribution-Free Uncertainty Quantification and Online Calibration
The flagship guarantee of this work is built on conformal prediction, which wraps any black-box predictor to produce prediction sets with finite-sample coverage. Classical split conformal prediction and its risk-controlling extensions, however, require exchangeability of the data, an assumption that an adaptive hopper reacting to jamming violates by construction. The relevant literature is therefore the online branch, whose guarantees hold for arbitrary, even adversarial, sequences. Adaptive Conformal Inference (ACI) of Gibbs and Candès [38] maintains coverage under unknown distribution shift by treating the quantile level as a single parameter updated online; its limitation — a hand-tuned step size — was removed by Gibbs and Candès&apos;s DtACI [39], which runs several step sizes in parallel and reweights them, and by Bhatnagar et al.&apos;s Strongly Adaptive Online Conformal Prediction (SAOCP) [40], which attains near-optimal strongly adaptive regret so that coverage is controlled on every sub-interval, not merely on average — the property that matters when an adversary concentrates misses right after a regime switch. Angelopoulos et al. [41] further show that decaying step sizes recover a population quantile when one exists while retaining the arbitrary-sequence guarantee. These online calibrators are the statistical core of our set-construction layer.
Controlling coverage is not the same as controlling decision loss. Conformal Risk Control (CRC) of Angelopoulos et al. [42] extends the conformal guarantee from miscoverage to the expected value of any monotone bounded loss, and subsequent work handles non-monotone losses [43] and tail risks such as conditional value-at-risk under adversarial drift [44]. The decisive step for our purpose is Conformal Decision Theory (CDT) of Lekeufack et al. [45], which calibrates the decision itself rather than a prediction set: a conformal controller adjusts a single scalar knob by the update λ_{t+1} = λ_t + η(α − ℓ_t), guaranteeing that long-run average loss stays at or below the target α for any sequence. This is exactly the mechanism by which our allocator&apos;s conservatism is tuned, and it directly calibrates the quantity we care about — jamming effect forgone — rather than a coverage proxy. For monitoring regime change, Bayesian online change-point detection (BOCPD) and CUSUM supply classical alarms, while anytime-valid inference via e-processes and conformal e-detectors provides distribution-free alarms with average-run-length control on the same score stream, consistent with the arbitrary-sequence philosophy of the rest of the layer.
Gap. Conformal and anytime-valid methods have been applied to forecasting, vision, language, and robot motion planning [45], but not, to our knowledge, to electronic-attack decision-making or to RF resource allocation under a declared risk budget. No prior work controls the realized effect of a jamming action against an adaptive emitter with a distribution-free guarantee, nor couples such a guarantee to the allocation of a physical power budget.
D. Worst-Case Evaluation, Emitter Sorting, and Sequence Backbones
When the adversary reacts to our actions, the next-hop distribution becomes a function of our own policy, and the passive-prediction assumption breaks. The right frame is the leader–follower (Stackelberg) game, equivalently the performative prediction setting of Perdomo et al. [46], in which deploying a model shifts the very distribution it is evaluated on and the performative optimum coincides with a Stackelberg equilibrium. Evaluating a policy in such a setting demands a worst-case certificate rather than average-case reward. Policy-Space Response Oracles (PSRO) of Lanctot et al. [47] provide this by growing a population of policies, each a best response to the opponent&apos;s meta-distribution, and reporting exploitability — how much a fresh best-response adversary can gain — as the certificate; Global PSRO [48] minimizes population exploitability directly and reaches a low-exploitability solution in fewer iterations, which matters when each iteration trains a fresh reinforcement-learning hopper. This supplies the exploitability certificate our evaluation reports against a held-out red team.
Because interleaved emitters must be separated before any per-emitter prediction, our sorting layer draws on multi-target tracking and deinterleaving for signals intelligence. The classical approach pairs a Kalman filter with multiple-hypothesis tracking to associate pulses to emitters, though extended-Kalman pulse-train deinterleaving has known limits under model mismatch. Recent work recasts sorting as graph learning: graph-convolutional and semi-supervised methods deinterleave radar pulses under heavy missing-pulse and spurious-pulse conditions [49], and temporal graph neural networks combine learned association with Kalman tracking for multi-target scenarios [50]. Our track-aware graph-attention sorter with a per-track Kalman predictor follows this line while adding moving-emitter dynamics that static similarity methods ignore. Finally, the sequence backbone itself — the selective state-space Mamba-2 model [31], [32] built on the S4 structured-state-space lineage [30] — provides linear-time inference over long hop histories under a per-slot latency budget, with a CNN-GRU [27] fallback.
Gap. Anti-jamming and EW reinforcement-learning work rarely reports a worst-case exploitability certificate, defaulting instead to average hit-rate or throughput against a fixed or self-authored adversary [8]–[15]; emitter sorting is typically evaluated on static emitters rather than moving ones whose bearing and power drift [37], [49]; and no open, configurable FH-EW simulation environment with a dumb-to-adaptive adversary library and a passive-sensor model has been released, leaving each study to its own bespoke setup.
E. Synthesis and Positioning
The four gaps converge on a single unoccupied position. Learning-based FH jamming optimizes a single channel or coordinates bandwidth across nodes, but never sizes a multi-tone jam from a calibrated prediction under a power budget (II-A). FH predictors are per-sequence, clean-data, point estimators, not zero-shot, gap-robust, calibrated distributions (II-B). Online conformal and conformal-decision methods give distribution-free guarantees for arbitrary sequences but have not been applied to electronic-attack decisions or RF resource allocation (II-C). And worst-case, exploitability-certified evaluation, track-aware moving-emitter sorting, and an open FH-EW environment are each largely absent from the EW literature (II-D). This work closes the seam by coupling an online-calibrated, decision-aware hop predictor to a threshold-aware, budgeted multi-tone allocator with a distribution-free bound on jamming effect forgone, evaluated under an exploitability certificate. Because the surveyed venues are predominantly English-language and IEEE-indexed, we frame the central claim as novel to our knowledge rather than as a categorical first, and we treat the predictability boundary — where cryptographically keyed or entropy-maximizing hoppers defeat all prediction — not as a gap but as a characterized result.
